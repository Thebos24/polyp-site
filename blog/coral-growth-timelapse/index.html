How to make a coral growth timelapse with your iPhone

By Sander Bos · 20 November 2026

A coral timelapse is not a video you record. It is a handful of photos taken weeks apart, from the same spot, shown one after the other.

Corals grow too slowly to film. A video of a tank for an hour tells you nothing; the same coral photographed once a fortnight for a year tells you a lot. The catch is that the photos have to line up. If the angle, the distance or the light moves between shots, the coral seems to jump around and you cannot tell what grew and what just moved.

1. Pick one spot and one rhythm

Decide where you will stand (or hold the phone) and keep to it. A pencil mark on the outside of the glass, or a piece of tape on the stand, is enough. For the rhythm, every two weeks suits most corals. Fast SPS can take weekly photos, and slow LPS and softies do fine monthly. A steady rhythm matters more than a short one.

2. Keep the conditions the same

  • Light: the same lighting preset every time, and the same time of day if your lights have a schedule.
  • Flow: switch the pumps off for a moment so polyps and branches are still.
  • Distance and angle: flat to the glass, as close as you can get without touching the coral.
  • Zoom: if you zoom, use the same level every time.

3. Use the previous photo as a guide

This is the part that is hard to do by eye. Polyp's camera shows your last photo of that coral on the live view, faintly, on top of what the camera sees. Move the phone until the rock, the plug or a neighbouring coral lines up with the ghost image, then take the shot. You can change how strong the ghost is, or switch it off. If the previous photo was zoomed, a button in the camera sets the same zoom, so the overlay still fits.

4. Fix the colour the same way every time

A lighter or bluer photo in the middle of a series makes the coral look like it changed. Keep the same light setting, and if you edit, keep the edits small and consistent. In Polyp the Adjust screen has a Same as last photo button for exactly this. More on colour in how to photograph your corals with an iPhone.

5. Play it back

Open the coral in Polyp and tap Play growth. The photos play in date order like a flipbook, inside the app, and every card shows the day and the age of the coral.

Polyp does not make a video file. If you want one to share, the simplest way is the iPhone's own screen recording while the photos play. You can also have Polyp make a collage of a coral's timeline and share that as a picture.

You already have old photos

If you have been photographing your corals for years, your timelapse already exists. It is just buried in the camera roll. Polyp can import those photos and keep their original dates, so a coral you photographed in 2023 shows up with its real history instead of starting today. Imported photos will not line up as neatly as ones taken with the ghost overlay, but the growth still shows.

Quick version

  1. Same spot, same distance, same light.
  2. One photo every one to four weeks, for as long as you can.
  3. Line each shot up with the last one.
  4. Keep edits small and the same.
  5. Play the photos back in date order.
