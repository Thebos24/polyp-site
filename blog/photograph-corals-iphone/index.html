How to photograph your corals with an iPhone

By Sander Bos · 20 November 2026

Your tank looks great in person. In a photo it turns into a blue smear. That is the lights, not you.

Reef lights are heavy on blue, and a phone camera tries to make the picture look "normal" by pulling the colour the other way. Sometimes it works, often it half works, and every photo comes out slightly different. For a nice shot that is annoying. For a growth timeline it is worse, because you want the corals to be the only thing that changes between photos.

Here is what helps, starting with the things that cost nothing.

1. Clean the glass and stop the flow for a moment

Wipe the inside and outside of the front glass first. Salt creep and algae show up as haze in a photo. Then switch off the powerheads and wavemakers for a minute or two. Polyps and tentacles that are swaying look soft however well you focus. Remember to switch everything back on.

2. Get close, and stay square to the glass

Hold the phone flat against the glass or a few centimetres away, pointing straight in. An angle gives you reflections and a stretched look. If you want to see more of the coral, move closer or use a bit of zoom. Pinching all the way in loses detail, so a little zoom goes a long way.

3. Tap to focus, then lock it

On an iPhone, touch and hold the coral in the standard Camera app until "AE/AF lock" appears. The phone now keeps that focus and brightness instead of hunting while you press the shutter. If the photo is too dark or too bright, slide the little sun icon up or down. Bright, fluorescent corals fool the exposure more often than you would expect, so check the result and try again.

4. Deal with the blue light

This is the hard part, and reefers disagree about how far to go. Three common approaches:

  • Turn the blue channels down for a minute. Many lights let you switch to a whiter preset or dim the blues while you shoot. Corals look closer to what you see by eye, and the photo needs less fixing.
  • Use an orange or yellow filter. Clip-on lenses and filters for phones cut the blue before it reaches the camera. Reef photographers often suggest orange for very blue lighting and yellow for white-blue lights, and say to try both on your own tank.
  • Correct it afterwards. Shoot first, adjust colour second. This is the easiest route, and it works well for most tanks.

Be honest with yourself about what the camera can do. Even cameras costing much more than a phone struggle with some reef lighting, and orange and red corals are the hardest to get right. Some reefers prefer the blue look and leave it alone, which is also fine.

5. Edit lightly

A little correction goes a long way. If you push the saturation, the corals look like a poster and you can no longer see whether they have changed. A good edit does two things: it removes the blue cast and it makes the brightness match your other photos of that coral.

In my app, Polyp, the Adjust button on a photo gives you six sliders (exposure, contrast, saturation, vibrance, warmth and tint) and an Auto button that corrects the blue cast. I kept Auto gentle on purpose. The original file is never changed; the adjustment is stored next to it, so you can undo it at any time.

6. Do it the same way every time

If you want to see growth, consistency matters more than any single great shot. Same spot in the tank, same distance, same angle, same light setting, same time of day if your lights have a schedule. Polyp's camera shows a faint copy of your last photo of that coral on the screen (the ghost overlay), so you can line the new shot up with the old one. If the last photo was taken zoomed in, a button sets the same zoom.

The timeline in Polyp shows the days between shots and how old the coral is, so you can look back after a year and see what happened.

A short checklist

  1. Clean the glass, pause the flow.
  2. Phone flat to the glass, close to the coral.
  3. Hold on the coral to lock focus and exposure.
  4. Reduce the blue, or fix it afterwards.
  5. Keep the edit gentle.
  6. Repeat from the same spot next time.
